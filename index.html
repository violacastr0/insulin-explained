Three ways to take insulin, made simple.

A plain-language guide for people who are newly diagnosed, and the families helping them. Compare a vial and syringe, an insulin pen and an insulin pump, then talk with your care team about which fits your life.

Compare the tools

What insulin does

Insulin is a hormone that lets your body move sugar from your blood into your cells for energy. With diabetes, the body either doesn’t make enough insulin or can’t use it well.

Type 1

The body makes little or no insulin, so everyone with type 1 needs to take insulin every day.

Type 2

The body doesn’t use insulin well. It’s managed with healthy habits, medicines, and for some people, insulin.

Gestational

Develops during pregnancy. It usually goes away after birth but raises the chance of type 2 later.

Vial and syringe

Insulin is drawn from a small glass vial into a syringe marked in units, then injected just under the skin.

Good to know

  • Widely available and often the lowest-cost option
  • Some insulin types can be mixed in one syringe
  • Each dose is measured by hand, so it takes practice

Insulin pen

A pen holds a cartridge of insulin. You attach a fresh needle, turn the dose knob until the dose window shows your dose, then press the injection button.

Good to know

  • Small and easy to carry
  • Dialing a dose is simpler than measuring with a syringe
  • Often costs more, and different insulins can’t be mixed

Insulin pump

A small device that delivers insulin all day.

Insulin pump

A pump sends small amounts of rapid-acting insulin through thin tubing into a soft tube under the skin, held by an adhesive patch called an infusion set. You program extra insulin for meals.

Good to know

  • Fewer injections; the infusion set is changed every few days
  • Some pumps work with a glucose sensor to adjust insulin
  • Needs training, and you wear the device most of the time
